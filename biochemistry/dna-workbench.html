The molecule

Strand builder

A DNA strand is a chain of nucleotides joined by 3′,5′-phosphodiester bonds, so it has direction: a 5′ end and a 3′ end. Build a strand and the page writes its antiparallel partner, counts the hydrogen bonds, and checks Chargaff's rules.

New nucleotides are added to the 3′ end, the same direction DNA polymerase works. Click any base on the top strand to see its parts.

Key ideas

  • Each nucleotide has a phosphate, 2′-deoxyribose, and a base. Purines (A, G) attach to C-1′ through N-9; pyrimidines (C, T) through N-1.
  • Phosphodiester bonds link the 3′-OH of one nucleotide to the 5′ phosphate of the next. Sequences are written 5′→3′.
  • The two strands are antiparallel and complementary: A=T with two hydrogen bonds, G≡C with three.
  • Chargaff's rules (A = T, G = C) describe double-stranded DNA. A single strand on its own need not follow them.

The molecule

Double helix

Two antiparallel strands wind around a common axis with the sugar–phosphate backbones outside and the stacked base pairs inside. The offset between the backbones creates a major and a minor groove. Compare the three forms Lehninger describes.

Drag sideways to rotate · schematic, to scale
strand 1, 5′ at top strand 2, 3′ at top ATGC

Base pairs are colored from the first 22 bp of the β-globin sequence. Geometry uses the diameter, rise and base pairs per turn of each form.

Key ideas

  • B-DNA, the Watson–Crick structure, is right-handed, about 20 Å across, with 3.4 Å between stacked base pairs and about 10.5 bp per turn in solution.
  • Hydrophobic stacking of the bases and hydrogen bonding between pairs both stabilize the helix.
  • The major groove exposes more of each base's edge, so proteins can read the sequence there without unwinding DNA.
  • A-DNA is wider and more compact (favored in low-water conditions and RNA–DNA hybrids); Z-DNA is left-handed and slimmer.

The molecule

Melting

Heat separates the two strands. Because stacked bases absorb less UV light, absorbance at 260 nm rises as DNA melts (the hyperchromic effect). The midpoint of that rise is the melting temperature, tm.

A 20 bp stretch at this GC content. A=T pairs separate first.

tm is estimated with an empirical formula for long DNA: 81.5 + 16.6 log[Na⁺] + 0.41(%GC). Values above 100 °C are extrapolations.

Key ideas

  • Melting (denaturation) breaks hydrogen bonds and unstacks the bases; covalent bonds stay intact.
  • The transition is cooperative, so it happens over a narrow temperature range.
  • Higher GC content raises tm. Higher salt also raises it by shielding the negatively charged phosphates from each other.
  • Melting is reversible. Slow cooling lets complementary strands find each other and re-anneal, which is what PCR and hybridization rely on.

PCR

The polymerase chain reaction copies one stretch of DNA exponentially. Two primers mark the ends, and a heat-stable polymerase repeats three steps: denature, anneal, extend. Design primers against the β-globin template, set the program, and run it.

Result

Press Run PCR to see the product.

Key ideas

  • The forward primer matches the top strand; the reverse primer matches the reverse complement of the bottom-strand end. Both 3′ ends point toward each other.
  • Set the annealing temperature a few degrees below the lower primer tm. Too high and primers won't stay bound; too low and they bind partial matches.
  • Copies roughly double each cycle until reagents run low, then the curve plateaus.
  • A no-template control should stay blank. A band there means contamination or primer dimers.

The toolkit

Restriction digest & gel

Restriction endonucleases cut at short, usually palindromic sequences. Choose enzymes, predict the fragments from the map of the teaching plasmid pTEACH, then run the gel. DNA is negatively charged, so it moves toward the positive electrode, and smaller fragments move farther.

Mystery plasmid

A 5,000 bp plasmid was cut with EcoRI, HindIII, and both. Which map fits the gel?

Key ideas

  • Most type II restriction sites are 4–8 bp palindromes: the site reads the same 5′→3′ on both strands.
  • Staggered cuts leave short single-stranded "sticky" ends (5′ or 3′ overhangs); cuts straight across leave blunt ends.
  • On a circular plasmid, n cuts give n fragments. On a linear molecule, n cuts give n + 1.
  • Band brightness tracks mass, so a large fragment glows brighter than a small one, and two same-size fragments overlap into one bright band.

Key ideas

  • Cutting vector and insert with the same enzymes gives matching sticky ends; DNA ligase seals the backbone.
  • Antibiotic selection: only cells that took up a plasmid with ampR grow on ampicillin.
  • Blue–white screening: an insert in the cloning site disrupts lacZα, so no β-galactosidase forms to cleave X-gal. Colonies with an insert stay white.
  • Two different enzymes give directional cloning and keep the empty vector from closing on itself.

The toolkit

Sanger sequencing

DNA polymerase extends a primer along the template. A small fraction of fluorescently labeled dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs) are mixed in; they lack a 3′-OH, so each one that is incorporated ends its chain. Sorting the fragments by length reads the sequence one base at a time. Read three patients' β-globin sequences and find codon 6.

Fragments start just after a primer that binds upstream. The read covers β-globin bases 10–33 (codons 3–10), so the read has the same sequence as the coding strand.

Genotype at codon 6

Key ideas

  • Each reaction has a template, a primer, DNA polymerase, all four dNTPs, and a little of each fluorescent ddNTP.
  • Every fragment ends in a ddNTP whose dye color names the last base. Capillary electrophoresis separates fragments that differ by one nucleotide.
  • The read is complementary to the template strand and runs 5′→3′ from the primer.
  • In a diploid sample, two overlapping peaks at one position point to a heterozygote. Next-generation methods run millions of reads in parallel, but this logic still applies.

CRISPR–Cas9

Cas9 is a nuclease steered by a guide RNA. Its 20-nucleotide spacer pairs with the target DNA, but Cas9 only cuts if a PAM (5′-NGG-3′ for S. pyogenes Cas9) sits right next to the target. The cell then repairs the break, and the repair route decides the edit. Try to correct the sickle-cell mutation.

Key ideas

  • The guide's 20-nt spacer matches the protospacer on the strand that carries the PAM; it base-pairs with the opposite strand.
  • Cas9 makes a blunt double-strand break about 3 bp upstream of the PAM. A PAM can be on either strand.
  • Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) is fast but error-prone. Small insertions or deletions often shift the reading frame and knock a gene out.
  • Homology-directed repair (HDR) copies a supplied donor template and can make a precise change, mostly close to the cut. Donors usually also alter the PAM so Cas9 can't cut the repaired DNA again.
