The pathway

Pathway map

Gluconeogenesis turns two pyruvate into one glucose. It shares seven reversible enzymes with glycolysis and replaces the three irreversible ones: pyruvate kinase, phosphofructokinase-1 and hexokinase. Click any step to see its enzyme, location and free-energy change.

Key ideas

  • Gluconeogenesis happens mainly in the liver, with smaller contributions from the renal cortex and the small intestine.
  • Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis share seven near-equilibrium steps. The three steps with large negative ΔG are replaced by different enzymes.
  • Bypass 1 (pyruvate → PEP) uses pyruvate carboxylase and PEP carboxykinase. Bypass 2 is fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase. Bypass 3 is glucose 6-phosphatase.
  • Because the bypass enzymes differ from the glycolytic ones, the two pathways can be regulated independently.

The pathway

Why bypass?

A reaction can run backward only if its ΔG in the cell is close to zero. Seven glycolytic steps are near equilibrium. Three are not, and running them in reverse would be strongly uphill. Switch between the three views to see the problem and the fix.

From ΔG′° to K′eq

K′eq = e−ΔG′°/RT, R = 8.315 J/mol·K, T = 298 K

ΔG in the cell

ΔG = ΔG′° + RT ln Q at 37 °C. Concentrations are example values; drag them to see how far ΔG moves.

Key ideas

  • ΔG′° tells you the direction under standard conditions; the actual ΔG, set by concentrations, decides the direction in the cell.
  • Aldolase has ΔG′° = +23.8 kJ/mol yet runs both ways, because product concentrations keep its ΔG near zero.
  • Hexokinase, PFK-1 and pyruvate kinase have large negative ΔG in the cell. Reversing them would need ΔG of the same size, uphill.
  • The bypasses make each reverse step favorable by spending ATP or GTP (bypass 1) or by simple hydrolysis of a phosphate ester (bypasses 2 and 3).

The pathway

Compartments

Gluconeogenesis crosses three compartments: the mitochondrial matrix, the cytosol and the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum. The route out of the mitochondrion depends on the starting material, because the cytosol must get the NADH it needs for the glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase step. Step through both routes.

    Key ideas

    • Pyruvate carboxylase is mitochondrial, so pyruvate must enter the matrix first.
    • Oxaloacetate can't cross the inner mitochondrial membrane. It leaves as malate (pyruvate route) or after conversion to PEP (lactate route).
    • Exporting malate also exports reducing equivalents: cytosolic malate dehydrogenase makes the NADH that the GAPDH step consumes.
    • The last step happens in the ER lumen of liver and kidney cells. Muscle and brain have no glucose 6-phosphatase, so they can't release glucose.

    Fuel & cost

    What can become glucose?

    A precursor can feed gluconeogenesis only if its carbons arrive as pyruvate, a citric acid cycle intermediate, or a triose phosphate. Decide for each molecule, then check where it enters.

    Where they enter

    Each answer fills in its spot on this map.

    Key ideas

    • The main precursors in animals are lactate, glucogenic amino acids (especially alanine) and glycerol from triacylglycerols.
    • Acetyl-CoA can't give net glucose in animals: the pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction is irreversible, and the two carbons that enter the citric acid cycle leave as two CO₂.
    • So even-chain fatty acids can't be converted to glucose. Odd-chain fatty acids give a little, through propionyl-CoA → succinyl-CoA.
    • Leucine and lysine are the only amino acids that are exclusively ketogenic.

    Fuel & cost

    Energy ledger

    Making glucose costs more than glycolysis returns. Pick a starting material and step through the pathway, keeping track of ATP, GTP and NADH for one glucose.

    Key ideas

    • From pyruvate: 4 ATP, 2 GTP and 2 NADH per glucose. Glycolysis to pyruvate yields only 2 ATP and 2 NADH.
    • The extra cost is what makes gluconeogenesis irreversible in the cell, just as glycolysis is.
    • Starting from lactate, lactate dehydrogenase supplies the NADH, so no net NADH is needed.
    • Glycerol enters at dihydroxyacetone phosphate, above bypass 1 and the phosphoglycerate kinase step, so it skips the costly part of the pathway.

    Control

    Reciprocal regulation

    If PFK-1 and FBPase-1 (or pyruvate kinase and the pyruvate → PEP bypass) were fully active at once, the cell would cycle substrate around and burn ATP as heat. Allosteric effectors turn one enzyme up as they turn its partner down. Move the effectors and watch the net flux.

    A qualitative model: activities are relative (0–100%) and respond in the directions Lehninger describes.

    Key ideas

    • Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate activates PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1. It is the liver's main switch between the two pathways.
    • AMP activates PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1; ATP and citrate inhibit PFK-1.
    • Acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase and inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase, sending pyruvate toward glucose when fatty acids are being oxidized.
    • Substrate cycles that run a little in both directions make the net flux more sensitive to effectors, at the price of some ATP turned to heat.

    Control

    Fed vs fasted

    Hormones set the liver's direction. After a meal, insulin favors glycolysis and glycogen storage. Between meals, glucagon makes the liver a glucose exporter, first from glycogen and then increasingly by gluconeogenesis. Drag the clock through a fast.

    Schematic shares of the glucose entering the blood, based on the classic picture of a fasting human. Blood glucose itself stays close to 4–5 mM throughout.

    Key ideas

    • Glucagon raises cAMP and activates protein kinase A (PKA) in the liver.
    • PKA phosphorylates the bifunctional enzyme PFK-2/FBPase-2, switching it to its phosphatase form, so [F2,6BP] falls: PFK-1 slows and FBPase-1 is released.
    • PKA also inactivates pyruvate kinase L, and glucagon (through CREB) increases transcription of the PEPCK and glucose 6-phosphatase genes. Insulin does the opposite.
    • Liver glycogen covers the first hours of a fast. Within about a day it is largely used up and gluconeogenesis supplies most of the glucose.

    Control

    Cori and glucose–alanine cycles

    Muscle and liver share the work. Muscle runs glycolysis fast and exports lactate or alanine; the liver turns them back into glucose and returns it through the blood. The muscle gains ATP quickly, and the liver pays to rebuild the glucose.

    Key ideas

    • In vigorous exercise, muscle glycolysis outruns oxidative metabolism and lactate dehydrogenase regenerates NAD⁺; lactate goes to the liver.
    • Per glucose, muscle gains 2 ATP and the liver spends 6 high-energy phosphates (4 ATP + 2 GTP): the cycle costs the body 4 per turn.
    • In the glucose–alanine cycle, muscle transaminates pyruvate to alanine, which carries amino groups to the liver for urea synthesis.
    • The liver's carbon skeleton becomes glucose again, so muscle protein breakdown and glucose supply are linked during fasting.
