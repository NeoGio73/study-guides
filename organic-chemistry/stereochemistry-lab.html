How to read these models

JSmol displays complete molecular structures with explicit atoms and bonds. Colors identify elements. Labels 1–4 mark the first atom in each ranked group; C* marks the stereocenter. “Show all hydrogens” reveals the remaining H atoms; H attached to the stereocenter stays visible.

The models are generated 3D conformers with verified stereochemistry. They are not experimental structures, and a displayed conformation is not a unique shape or an energy comparison. In the H-away view, H can be hidden behind C*. Rotate slightly to reveal it.

For the chiral examples, C* is the molecule’s only stereocenter. In 2-propanol, the two CH₃ groups are equivalent, so this carbon has no R/S assignment. A mirror-image conformation can represent the same achiral compound.

Compare the atoms, not the camera angle

C2 and C3 labels follow the same atoms during rotation. Molecules can have the same configuration even when their views or conformations differ. Rotating the whole molecule cannot change R into S.

The symmetry check matters

This molecule has identical ends. Its (2R,3S) and (2S,3R) descriptions represent the same meso compound. Two stereocenters therefore give three distinct stereoisomers here, rather than four. A meso compound can adopt a conformation with internal symmetry; that symmetry may not be visually obvious in an arbitrary conformer.

Check the priorities at C2 and C3

At either center: Cl is priority 1; the neighboring CHCl carbon is 2; the terminal CH₃ carbon is 3; H is 4. For the carbon tie, [Cl, C, H] outranks [H, H, H]. These are 3D structures, so the H direction depends on the current view. Orient the lowest-priority group away before reading R/S.

The (2R,3R)/(2S,3S) pair are enantiomers. Either one compared with the meso compound gives diastereomers. For molecules with more than one stereocenter, check whole-molecule symmetry before deciding that inversion of every labeled center produces a distinct enantiomer.

Quick reference: keep these ideas separate

Chirality and relationships

Chiral: a molecule cannot be superimposed on its mirror image by rotation and translation.

Enantiomers: distinct, nonsuperimposable mirror-image stereoisomers.

Diastereomers: stereoisomers that are not mirror images.

Meso: an achiral compound that contains stereocenters. Internal symmetry can make the whole molecule achiral.

Constitutional isomers: same molecular formula, different connectivity. Check connectivity before stereochemistry.

Assignment and symmetry

CIP ties: compare attached atoms as lists sorted from highest to lowest atomic number. Continue outward to the first difference; do not add the atomic numbers. For this lab’s carbonyl examples, count a doubly bonded oxygen twice. For isotopes of one element, higher mass number ranks first.

R/S versus +/−: configuration does not predict the sign of optical rotation.

Racemic versus meso: a racemate is an equal mixture of two enantiomers; a meso compound is a single achiral compound.

2ⁿ: an upper bound for combinations of n independent tetrahedral stereocenters, not a guarantee of that many distinct stereoisomers.

Learning goals and model scope

Identify a tetrahedral carbon with four different groups; assign priorities using atomic number and the first point of difference; assign R/S with group 4 toward or away; distinguish rotation from configurational inversion; classify identical structures, enantiomers, diastereomers, and constitutional isomers; recognize a meso compound.

This activity focuses on tetrahedral carbon stereochemistry. It does not cover E/Z assignment, axial chirality, conformational energies, or reaction mechanisms. Absence of a tetrahedral stereocenter is not a universal test for achirality. The 3D coordinates were generated for these examples and checked for the stated absolute configurations. Priority tables are curated for the included molecules. No live chemical database search is needed.
