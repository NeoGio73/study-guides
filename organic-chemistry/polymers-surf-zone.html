A1

Words you need first

Monomer
The small molecule that is linked over and over (ethylene, styrene, adipic acid).
Polymer
The large molecule made of many repeat units. Polymer molar masses are averages, often 104–106 g/mol.
Repeat unit
The bracketed piece drawn with subscript n. The bonds that cross the brackets connect to the next unit.
Degree of polymerization (n)
Number of repeat units in a chain: n = (polymer molar mass) ÷ (repeat-unit molar mass).
Homopolymer / copolymer
One kind of monomer vs. two or more kinds in the same chain.
Chain-growth vs. step-growth
Monomers add one at a time to a reactive chain end, vs. any two molecules with the right functional groups react with each other.
Thermoplastic
Softens on heating and can be remolded (PE, PS, PET). Chains are not covalently linked to each other.
Thermoset
Covalently crosslinked into one network; it chars instead of melting (cured epoxy, cured polyester resin, vulcanized rubber).
Elastomer
Stretches and snaps back: lightly crosslinked chains above their Tg (rubber, neoprene, spandex).
Tg (glass transition)
Below it, the amorphous regions are rigid and glassy; above it they are rubbery.
Tm (melting)
Where the crystalline regions melt. Only partly crystalline polymers have one.
Crystallite
A region where chains pack in an ordered way. More crystallinity means stiffer, stronger, more opaque.
A2

Chain-growth polymerization

An initiator creates a reactive chain end: a radical, a cation, an anion, or a metal–carbon bond. Each step adds one alkene to that end, so long chains appear early while most monomer is still unreacted.

Radical mechanism (the most common route for vinyl monomers)

1Initiation
RO–OR heat or hν⟶ 2 RO•
RO• + CH2=CH–Ph ⟶ RO–CH2–CH–Ph

The weak O–O bond breaks homolytically. The radical adds to the CH2 end so the new radical is benzylic and resonance-stabilized.

2Propagation
R–CH2–CH–Ph + CH2=CH–Ph ⟶ R–CH2–CH(Ph)–CH2–CH–Ph

Each step consumes one monomer and regenerates the radical at the chain end. Repeated thousands of times, it builds the chain head to tail.

3Termination
Combination2 R–CH2–CH–Ph ⟶ R–CH2–CH(Ph)–CH(Ph)–CH2–R
Disproportionation2 R–CH2–CH–Ph ⟶ R–CH2–CH2–Ph + R–CH=CH–Ph

Two radicals meet. They either bond to each other, or one takes a hydrogen atom from the other, leaving one saturated end and one alkene end.

4Chain transfer
R• + H–C(chain) ⟶ R–H + •C(chain)

A growing radical removes a hydrogen atom from the middle of another chain. A new chain grows from that point, which creates branching (as in LDPE).

Head-to-tail regiochemistry. The radical adds to the CH2 end so the new radical sits on the substituted carbon, where it is more stable (benzylic, tertiary, or stabilized by an ester or nitrile). Substituents therefore land on every other carbon.
Initiators. Benzoyl peroxide and AIBN have weak bonds (O–O, or C–N with loss of N2) that break homolytically. In surfboard resin the initiator is MEKP, a peroxide, activated by a cobalt salt at room temperature (Part B).
LDPE vs. HDPE. High-pressure radical polymerization of ethylene gives many branches from chain transfer, so chains pack poorly: low density, soft, clear film. Ziegler–Natta catalysts give linear chains that pack into crystallites: high density, stiff milk jugs.

A4

Tacticity: stereochemistry along a chain

Every CH(CH3) carbon in polypropylene is a stereocenter. Drawn on a zigzag backbone, the methyl groups can all point the same way, alternate, or point randomly. Ziegler–Natta catalysts make the isotactic form, which crystallizes well (Tm ≈ 165 °C). Atactic polypropylene cannot pack and is a soft, sticky solid.

Conjugated dienes polymerize by 1,4-addition: the two outer carbons form the new chain bonds and a C=C is left in the middle of each repeat unit. That remaining double bond can be cis or trans, and it gives a site for crosslinking.

A7

Step-growth polymerization

Two different bifunctional monomers (or one monomer with two different groups) react through ordinary functional-group chemistry. Many lose a small molecule (H2O, HCl, CH3OH) each time a bond forms, so they are also called condensation polymers. Chains stay short until almost every group has reacted: at 99% conversion, the average chain is only about 100 units long.

How long do the chains get? The Carothers equation

Let p be the extent of reaction, the fraction of functional groups that have reacted. When the two monomers are mixed in an exact 1:1 ratio, the number-average degree of polymerization is:

X¯n=11−p Mn=X¯n×M0 Đ=MwMn=1+p X̄n counts monomer units, so one nylon 6,6 repeat unit counts as 2. M0 = average molar mass per monomer unit (113.16 g/mol for nylon 6,6). Đ, the dispersity, tells how spread out the chain lengths are; for step growth it approaches 2.

Half the groups have reacted and the average "chain" is a dimer. A strong fiber needs Mn of roughly 10,000 g/mol or more, so the reaction must run past 99%. Getting the ratio exactly right matters just as much. If r is the ratio of the scarcer group to the more plentiful one (r ≤ 1):

X¯n=1+r1+r−2rpAt p = 1 this becomes (1 + r)/(1 − r). Just 1% extra diamine (r = 0.99) caps the chains at 199 units, however long the reaction runs: every chain end becomes an amine with nothing left to react with. Manufacturers first make "nylon salt", the 1:1 ammonium salt of hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid, to lock in the exact ratio.

Sun, heat and water. UV light breaks bonds and forms radicals in cured polyester resin, which is why white boards yellow with age. PU foam is mostly closed-cell. EPS is built from beads with tiny channels between them, so a cracked EPS board can soak up water. Epoxy boards often have a small vent plug because a hot car makes the air inside expand.
Industry history. In December 2005 Clark Foam, which supplied most of the polyurethane blanks in the U.S., shut down abruptly, citing regulatory pressure over its chemicals, including the diisocyanate TDI. The shortage sped up the move to EPS/epoxy boards and to cleaner polyurethane formulas, and today some shapers use bio-based resins and recycled foam.

Where the plastic goes

Sunlight breaks the chains. UV light and oxygen pull hydrogen atoms off the backbone. The carbon radicals that form react with O2, the chains break (chain scission) and pick up C=O groups, and the plastic turns brittle and cracks into smaller and smaller pieces. Pieces under 5 mm are microplastics. Polypropylene breaks down faster in sunlight than polyethylene: every other carbon in PP carries a tertiary C–H, which is weaker because losing that H gives a more stable 3° radical. That is the same radical-stability order you use in Organic 1.
Thermosets can't be remelted. A PET bottle or an HDPE jug is a thermoplastic that can be melted and molded again. A cured polyester or epoxy board is one crosslinked network, so at the end of its life it can only be ground up, burned or landfilled. That is why surfboards are so hard to recycle, and why some shapers now use recycled foam blanks, bio-based resins, or test designs that can be taken apart.

Whether a piece of plastic floats depends on its density compared with seawater (about 1.025 g/mL). Floating plastics drift with the currents and wash up on beaches, including the small pre-production pellets ("nurdles") found on Florida sand. Dense plastics sink into the sediment.

Why a wetsuit keeps you warm, even in Florida

Water conducts heat about 24 times better than air (thermal conductivity k ≈ 0.60 vs 0.026 W/m·K), and moving water carries heat away faster still. Neoprene foam is full of tiny closed gas bubbles, so its conductivity is close to that of a gas: about 0.05 W/m·K. Heat flow through the suit follows Fourier's law:

q=kAΔTdq = heat flow (W), A = skin area (about 1.8 m² for an adult), ΔT = skin temperature minus water temperature, d = thickness. Double the thickness and you halve the heat loss. A "3/2" suit is 3 mm on the torso and 2 mm on the arms and legs.

C1

Anatomy of a wave

Height H · trough to crest. Surf reports often quote the significant height Hs, the average of the highest third of waves.
Wavelength L · crest to crest. Period T · seconds between crests passing one point. For surfers, period is the most important number on a buoy report.
Wind sea vs. groundswell · local wind makes short-period (≲ 8 s), choppy waves. Waves that travel far from a storm sort themselves out into long-period (10–20 s), evenly spaced groundswell.

How fast waves travel

Deep water (d > L/2): L0=gT22π≈1.56T2 C0=gT2π≈1.56T Cg=C02 SI units: m, s, m/s. Cg is the group speed, the speed of the wave energy and of a set of waves.
Shallow water (d < L/20): c=gd Speed depends only on depth. In between, use the full dispersion relation ω2 = gk tanh(kd).

Longer-period waves travel faster in deep water, so a distant storm's swell arrives in order: the longest periods first (the "forerunners"), then shorter ones. A hurricane's swell can reach Florida a day or more before the storm's clouds do.

What sets wave height out at sea

Three things decide how big wind-made waves grow: wind speed U, fetch F (the distance of open water the wind blows over), and duration. Height grows roughly with U2. If the wind blows long enough over a long enough fetch, the sea becomes "fully developed" and stops growing.

C3

Bathymetry: the bottom shapes the wave

Bathymetry is the shape and depth of the sea floor. Once the depth is less than about half a wavelength, a wave "feels" the bottom and changes:

Shoaling. The wave slows, its wavelength shrinks, and the same energy is squeezed into a shorter, taller wave. In shallow water, Green's law gives H2H1=(d1d2)1/4.
Refraction. The part of a crest in shallower water slows first, so crests bend toward shallow ground, just as light bends in glass. Snell's law: sinθ1c1=sinθ2c2. Shoals, reefs and points focus energy (bigger waves). Underwater canyons spread it out (smaller waves).
Reflection and interference. A wave that bounces off a jetty can cross the next incoming wave. Where crests line up, heights add. Sebastian Inlet's famous "wedge" next to the north jetty forms this way.
Sandbars and inlets. Florida's east coast is mostly sand, so the "reef" is a sandbar that moves after every storm. Inlets build shallow sand deltas at their mouths, which is one reason the waves near Ponce Inlet (New Smyrna Beach) are often better shaped than the open beach.

Two famous bottoms, two different monsters

Mavericks (Half Moon Bay, California). A bedrock reef that rises out of deep water along an unusually long, gradual ramp. Its abrupt rocky ridge works like a lens: refraction bends the crests in and focuses winter North Pacific swell onto one spot, so the wave grows far taller than the swell around it before it pitches forward. Mavericks is mainly about height: shoaling plus focusing over a ramp. More detail: Surfline, "Mechanics of Maverick's".▶Play Mavericks Big WaveSurf this reef on four real big-swell days. The seafloor comes from NOAA's survey, so the wave stands up over the same ridge the map shows. Paddle in, make the drop, and watch your speed and the wave height. Just for fun; works on phones too.
Teahupo'o (Tahiti). Deep ocean comes within a couple of hundred meters of the reef, so long-period swell shoals almost all at once. A flatter shelf lets the wave stand up, then the coral rises again under it and it breaks so suddenly that the lip throws out and lands in a trough below sea level. A deep channel beside the reef lets it peel instead of closing out. Teahupo'o is mainly about shape: the reef rises so abruptly from deep water that the whole swell trips at once and throws an extreme plunging barrel, thick rather than tall.▶Play Teahupo'o Big WaveSurf the 27 August 2011 "Code Red" swell on a reef built from the French Navy's seabed survey (SHOM). When a wave breaks, and when it barrels

A wave breaks when its height becomes a large fraction of the water depth (crest particles then move faster than the wave itself). Whether it spills gently or throws out a hollow tube depends on how fast the bottom rises compared to how steep the wave is. That ratio is the Iribarren number (surf similarity parameter).

Speed grows only with the square root of length: making a board 50% longer adds about 22% to hull speed. A longer board also reaches wave speed sooner, so longboards catch small, slow waves earlier than shortboards.

Width sets stability (and costs speed)

Tipping stability comes from the second moment of area of the waterline outline, which scales with width cubed. The metacentric radius BM measures how strongly the board resists rolling:

I≈CwLW312 BM=IVsub Cw ≈ 0.6 for a board outline (it is not a rectangle). Vsub = submerged volume = total mass ÷ water density.

Going from a 29″ to a 32″ SUP, length unchanged: (32/29)3 ≈ 1.34, so about 34% more resistance to tipping. The price is more wetted surface and a wider bow pushing water aside, so more drag. Race boards are long and narrow (length about 6–7 times width); beginner boards are shorter and wide (about 4 times).

Volume sets flotation (Archimedes)

FB=ρwaterVsubgSeawater ρ ≈ 1.025 kg/L, so each liter of board can hold up about 1 kg before it is fully underwater.

Common rules of thumb: a beginner SUP of roughly 1.5–2 L per kg of rider; an advanced surfer's shortboard of about 0.35–0.45 L per kg. A shortboard barely floats its rider while sitting still, which is why it only works once the wave provides speed.

Speed on the wave

Once on the wave, the board is not limited by hull speed. Gravity pulls it down the sloping face (acceleration ≈ g sin θ for face angle θ), the moving water face keeps rebuilding under it, and the board planes: lift from water flowing under the bottom raises it so less of it is in the water.

Riding straight in:v≈c=gdb Angling across the face at angle α:v≈ccosα Planing lift:FL=12ρv2ACL

A 1.5 m wave breaks in about 1.9 m of water, so it moves at √(9.81 × 1.9) ≈ 4.3 m/s (about 10 mph). Trimming across the face at 60° doubles that to about 8.6 m/s (19 mph), twice what any board can paddle. Because lift grows with v2, a small, short board planes easily at wave speed, and its small wetted area makes it fast and quick to turn. Rocker (the bottom's curve from nose to tail) costs some speed but keeps the nose from digging in on a steep face.

Fluid dynamics: bottoms, rails and fins

A board is a moving shape in a fluid, just like a boat hull or an airplane wing. Water flowing under the bottom holds it up. Water flowing past the fins lets it turn and keeps it from sliding sideways. Both follow the same rules: pressure and lift grow with the square of speed, and how the water leaves the board matters as much as how it arrives.

Laminar or turbulent? The Reynolds number

Re=vLνν = kinematic viscosity of seawater ≈ 1.05 × 10−6 m²/s. Above roughly 5 × 105 along a surface, the boundary layer turns turbulent.

A 2 m board at 5 m/s: Re ≈ (5 × 2) / 1.05 × 10−6 ≈ 107, fully turbulent. A fin with an 11 cm chord at the same speed: Re ≈ 5 × 105, right at the transition. That is why fin shape and surface finish matter so much, and why a waxy, smooth, ding-free bottom is faster than a scratched one.

Bottom contours

Flat. The most planing area for its size. Fast and simple, but water escapes sideways, so it grips less in turns.
Single concave. A shallow channel down the middle traps water and raises the pressure under the board (Bernoulli: the flow slows and is redirected downward), adding lift. More lift means the board rides higher and faster with less wetted area. Common in shortboards.
Double concave. Two smaller channels inside the single concave, usually toward the tail. They make it easier to roll from one rail to the other while keeping some lift.
Vee. The bottom rises from the center toward the rails, like a shallow boat keel. It lets the board tip onto a rail easily, so it suits wide, heavy longboards and SUPs, at a small cost in speed.
Rocker. The curve of the bottom from nose to tail. A flat rocker planes early and is fast in small waves. More rocker fits a steep, curving wave face and turns tighter, but it pushes more water (more drag).
Rails and release. Soft, rounded rails let water wrap around them and grip the wave face. Hard, sharp edges near the tail let water break away cleanly, so it releases without dragging. Channels and tail shape (round, squash, pin, swallow) control how much area sits at the back, where the board pivots.

Fins are underwater wings

A fin meeting the water at a small angle of attack α works like a wing turned on its side. It makes a sideways lift force that resists sliding and pushes the board around a turn. Too large an angle and the flow separates from the fin: it stalls, and the tail slides out.

L=12ρv2ACL CL≈2παARAR+2 AR=depth2area α in radians. The AR factor corrects for a short, stubby fin, where water leaks around the tip and creates a tip vortex and induced drag. Good up to about 12–15°, where most fins begin to stall.
Single fin. One big fin far back: smooth, stable, draws long turns. Classic longboards and many SUPs.
Twin. Two side fins: loose and fast down the line, less hold in steep, powerful waves.
Thruster (3). Twin plus a center fin: the most common setup, balancing drive, hold and control.
Quad and 2+1. Four fins push speed and grip on the rail. A 2+1 (big center, small sides) is the standard SUP and longboard combination.

Side fins are toed in (pointed slightly toward the nose) so they already have an angle of attack, and canted (tilted outward) so they stay vertical when the board leans onto a rail. Their inside face is often flat and the outside curved, like a wing, to make lift in one direction. Rake (how far the tip sweeps back) trades quick pivoting for long, drawn-out turns.

Torque: why fin position matters

A force acting at a distance from a pivot makes a torque: τ=rFsinθ. Fins create two torques worth knowing.

Yaw (steering) torque on the board. The fin's lift acts far behind the rider's feet, so it twists the whole board around the rider. A fin farther back has a longer lever arm: the board tracks straighter and is harder to turn. Moving fins forward shortens the arm and makes the board looser. This is the physics behind sliding a SUP or longboard fin forward or back in its box.
Bending torque at the fin base. The same lift acts at the fin's center of pressure, about 40% of the way down from the base. Lift × that height is the bending moment the fin box must resist. Deep fins and hard, fast turns multiply it, which is why fins snap at the base and fin boxes crack out of the board, and why boxes are glassed in with extra layers of fiberglass.

East Florida: why the surf does what it does

Wind direction

  • Offshore (W, SW, NW): blows from land to sea, grooms the faces smooth and holds them up so they break later and hollower.
  • Onshore (E, NE, SE): blows waves over early, adds chop, crumbles the shape.
  • Side-shore (N, S): in between; light side-shore is often fine.
  • Sea breeze: on warm days land heats faster than water, the air over land rises, and cooler ocean air flows in from the east by afternoon. Morning is often glassy; afternoon turns onshore.
Nor'easters (fall through spring). A low-pressure system off the coast with high pressure to the north squeezes strong NE winds over hundreds of miles of ocean. Lots of fetch means lots of wave energy, but the wind is onshore, so the surf is big, short-period and messy. The best day is often the one after: the low moves out, the wind swings NW (offshore), and the leftover swell cleans up.
Hurricanes (June–November, peak Aug–Oct). The ideal storm stays far out in the Atlantic, east or southeast of Florida, and curves north. It sends long-period groundswell (often 12–16 s) while the local wind stays light or offshore. A storm that comes close brings dangerous rip currents, flooding, beach erosion and onshore wind. No surf is worth that.
The Bahamas shadow. The Bahama Islands and banks block much of the swell coming from the east and southeast for the coast south of about Palm Beach. Central and North Florida are more open to the Atlantic.
The continental shelf. Off Palm Beach the shelf edge is close to shore. To the north it widens, and Cape Canaveral has extensive offshore shoals. Waves crossing a wide, shallow shelf lose energy to bottom friction before they reach the beach, and shoals refract and shadow them.
Tides. Two highs and two lows a day, with an average range of about 4 ft (1.2 m) at Daytona Beach. On a shallow sandbar that is a big share of the total depth. See the tide lab below.
Cold fronts (winter). Winds blow from the south ahead of the front, swing west and northwest behind it, then NE as high pressure builds. That sequence (onshore swell, then offshore clean-up) is a common winter pattern.
The Gulf Stream. This strong northward current runs offshore. Swell from the north running against it steepens, part of why the offshore water can be rough even on modest days.
Rip currents. Water pushed over a sandbar by breaking waves returns seaward through gaps in the bar as a narrow, fast current. Caught in one? Swim parallel to the beach, then in. Surfers use rips as a free ride out; swimmers should stay away.
Lightning. Summer afternoon storms make Florida a lightning hot spot. A surfer in the water is the tallest object on a flat sea. Get out when you hear thunder.

Tides: why the same swell can be great at 9 a.m. and gone by noon

The Moon's gravity, and to a lesser degree the Sun's, pulls the ocean into two bulges that the Earth rotates through, so Florida's Atlantic coast gets a high and a low tide roughly every 12 hours 25 minutes. When the Sun and Moon line up (new and full moon) the range is largest: spring tides. At the quarter moons it is smallest: neap tides. Fall's extra-high "king tides" add the Moon's closest approach.

h(t)=R2(1−cos2πt12.42)Water level above low tide, for tide range R, t hours after low tide. Sailors' shortcut, the rule of twelfths: in the six hours from low to high the water rises 1, 2, 3, 3, 2, 1 twelfths of the range, fastest at mid-tide.
Depth controls breaking. A wave breaks when H ≈ 0.78 d. Add 4 ft of tide over a sandbar that is 3 ft deep at low tide and the depth more than doubles. Waves that peeled perfectly on the bar at low or mid tide may now roll over it and dump on the shore instead. Many Florida beach breaks work best on a rising mid tide; at dead low, some close out on the bar all at once.
Tidal currents at inlets. At Ponce, Sebastian and other inlets, the outgoing (ebb) tide rushes seaward against incoming waves. Waves running into an opposing current slow down, shorten and steepen, so the inlet mouth gets bigger and more powerful, and more dangerous. On the incoming (flood) tide, clean ocean water flows in and the current runs with the waves.

E · Biology

Seasons, water temperature and sharks

The same seasons that bring nor'easters and hurricane swell also move animals up and down the coast. Most sharks are ectotherms: their body temperature follows the water, so their metabolism, digestion and swimming speed rise and fall with it. Their migrations track water temperature and the movements of the fish they eat.

White sharks (great whites). In winter, tagged white sharks gather on the continental shelf from North Carolina to Florida's east coast, an area OCEARCH calls the Northwest Atlantic Shared Foraging Area. Detections are highest off Charleston, SC and Cape Canaveral, FL. A wedge of cooler water trapped between the Gulf Stream and the coast runs from the Outer Banks down to Cape Canaveral, giving a wide range of temperatures close together that may concentrate prey. In spring they head north toward Cape Cod and Atlantic Canada for summer and fall. They stay mostly offshore on the shelf, not in the surf zone.
Blacktip sharks. Every winter blacktips migrate down the Atlantic coast. They reach southeast Florida in January, peak in mid-February, and turn north in March or April toward mating areas off Georgia and the Carolinas. The winter schools are more than 95% male. Florida Atlantic University aerial surveys show the nearshore counts have fallen to about one-tenth of earlier levels, which researchers link to warming water: the sharks' preferred winter temperatures are now found farther north.
Surf-zone sharks and the mullet run. In late summer and fall, huge schools of mullet move south along the beach, and spinner and blacktip sharks, tarpon and other predators follow them into the surf zone. Volusia County, especially around New Smyrna Beach, logs some of the highest numbers of unprovoked shark bites in the world. Nearly all are minor bites from small sharks that mistake a hand or foot for a fish in murky, splashing water.
A warm-bodied exception. White sharks are regional endotherms. A rete mirabile, a dense web of arteries and veins running side by side, works as a countercurrent heat exchanger: warm blood leaving the swimming muscles heats the cold blood coming in from the gills. Their muscles, eyes and brain stay warmer than the water, which lets them hunt in cold northern seas in summer.

Temperature and metabolism: the Q10 rule

For an ectotherm, metabolic rate rises by a roughly constant factor, Q10, for every 10 °C of warming. Q10 is usually 2–3 for animals, the same idea as reaction rates rising with temperature in chemical kinetics.

R2R1=Q10(T2−T1)/10A shark moving from 20 °C winter water to 28 °C summer water with Q10 = 2.5 burns energy about 2.1 times as fast.

How the sharks are tracked

Satellite (SPOT) tags. Bolted to the dorsal fin. When the fin breaks the surface, the tag transmits to a satellite, which calculates a position. Sharks that stay deep can go days or weeks without a "ping," so tracks have gaps.
Acoustic tags. A small transmitter implanted in the shark sends a coded sound pulse. Underwater receivers along the coast log each shark that swims past, and researchers share detections across networks. They give no live map, but they record sharks that rarely surface.
Live tracker. OCEARCH posts the latest satellite pings for its tagged white sharks, tiger sharks, turtles and other animals on a public map at ocearch.org/tracker, also available as the free OCEARCH Shark Tracker app. This page cannot embed the map, so the link opens it in a new tab. Zoom to the coast between Jacksonville and Cape Canaveral, set the date filter to the last 30 days, and note which sharks have pinged nearby. Then check again in a different season and compare.
Common-sense safety. Avoid the water at dawn and dusk, near schools of baitfish or diving birds, near fishing piers and inlet mouths when the water is murky, and with shiny jewelry. Leave if you see a shark.

Name the polymer. Which reactive intermediates can polymerize its monomer, and why? How does it connect to a SUP paddle?

Isobutylene polymerizes by a cationic route but not an anionic one. Explain.
H⁺ adds to the CH₂ end to give a tertiary carbocation, stabilized by hyperconjugation from three alkyl groups. Alkyl groups donate electrons and would destabilize a carbanion.
A shaper tries to glass an EPS foam blank with polyester resin. What happens, and what should they use?
The styrene in polyester resin dissolves polystyrene foam (like dissolves like), and the core melts away. Use epoxy resin, which contains no styrene.
Cured epoxy, PET and HDPE: which is a thermoset? Which would have a recycling code? Explain using chain structure.
Cured epoxy is a thermoset: a covalent 3-D network that cannot melt. PET (code 1) and HDPE (code 2) are thermoplastics: separate chains that soften on heating and can be remolded.
